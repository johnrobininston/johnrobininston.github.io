1 Diffusion Equation

References: Evans, Partial Differential Equations, Section 2.3; Strauss, Partial Differential Equations, Chapter 2; Crank, The Mathematics of Diffusion.

The diffusion equation describes how a conserved quantity (concentration, heat, probability) spreads from regions of high to low density. The constant-coefficient case is the Heat Equation, which is treated there in detail. This note covers the general form with variable diffusivity, drift and reaction terms, its microscopic origin, and its link to Brownian Motion and the Fokker-Planck Equation.

Let \(u(t,x)\) be the density of a quantity in \(\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^n\). The diffusion equation is \[ u_t=\nabla\cdot\left(D(x)\nabla u\right), \] where \(D(x)>0\) is the diffusion coefficient (diffusivity). When \(D\) is a constant this is \(u_t=D\Delta u\), the heat equation with \(k=D\).

The equation is the combination of two ingredients:

  1. a conservation law \(u_t+\nabla\cdot\vec J=0\); and
  2. a constitutive law \(\vec J=-D\nabla u\) (Fick’s first law).

2 Derivation

2.1 From Conservation of Mass

Let \(\Omega'\subset\Omega\) be any subregion. The mass changes only through flux across its boundary, so by the divergence theorem \[ \frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega'}u~dx=-\int_{\partial\Omega'}\vec J\cdot\vec n~dS =-\int_{\Omega'}\nabla\cdot\vec J~dx. \] Since \(\Omega'\) is arbitrary, \(u_t+\nabla\cdot\vec J=0\). Fick’s law \(\vec J=-D\nabla u\) states that the flux points down the gradient with strength proportional to its steepness, giving \(u_t=\nabla\cdot(D\nabla u)\).

2.2 From a Random Walk

Consider a particle on the lattice \(h\mathbb{Z}\) which, every \(\tau\) units of time, jumps one step left or right with probability \(1/2\). If \(p(t,x)\) is the probability of being at \(x\) at time \(t\) then \[ p(t+\tau,x)=\tfrac12p(t,x+h)+\tfrac12p(t,x-h). \] Subtracting \(p(t,x)\) and Taylor expanding, \[ \frac{p(t+\tau,x)-p(t,x)}{\tau}=\frac{h^2}{2\tau}\cdot \frac{p(t,x+h)+p(t,x-h)-2p(t,x)}{h^2}. \] Taking \(h,\tau\to0\) with \(h^2/(2\tau)\to D\) fixed gives \(p_t=Dp_{xx}\). The diffusive scaling \(h\sim\sqrt\tau\) is what produces a non-trivial limit and is the discrete version of the scaling of Brownian motion.

Note: The microscopic derivation shows that \(D\) has units of length\(^2\)/time, and that diffusion is the macroscopic shadow of independent random motion of many particles. The equation is therefore also the law of the probability density of a single particle.

3 Generalisations

Adding a velocity field \(\vec V\) and a source/reaction term \(R(u)\), \[ u_t+\nabla\cdot(\vec Vu)=\nabla\cdot(D\nabla u)+R(u). \]

The terms are, in order, the time derivative, advection (transport, see Transport Equation), diffusion and reaction. Important cases:

  • Pure advection-diffusion (\(R=0\)): \(u_t+\vec V\cdot\nabla u=D\Delta u\) for constant \(\vec V\) with \(\nabla\cdot\vec V=0\).
  • Fisher-KPP equation (\(R=ru(1-u)\)): logistic growth plus diffusion, producing travelling population fronts with speed \(2\sqrt{rD}\).
  • Non-linear diffusion (\(D=D(u)\)): e.g. the porous medium equation \(u_t=\Delta(u^m)\), which has finite speed of propagation.
  • Anisotropic diffusion: \(D\) is a positive definite matrix and the equation is \(u_t=\nabla\cdot(D\nabla u)\) with \(D=D(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\).

The equation \(u_t+vu_x=Du_{xx}\) with constant \(v\) is solved from the heat kernel by moving to the frame \(y=x-vt\). Setting \(u(t,x)=w(t,x-vt)\) gives \(w_t=Dw_{yy}\), so \[ u(t,x)=\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}} e^{-(x-vt-y)^2/4Dt}u_0(y)~dy. \] The cloud translates at speed \(v\) while spreading like \(\sqrt{2Dt}\).

4 Link with Stochastic Processes

Let \(X_t\) solve the SDE \(dX_t=\mu(X_t)~dt+\sigma(X_t)~dW_t\). Its transition density \(p(t,x)\) solves the Fokker-Planck (forward Kolmogorov) equation \[ p_t=-\nabla\cdot(\mu p)+\frac12\sum_{i,j}\partial_{ij}\left((\sigma\sigma^\top)_{ij}p\right). \] With \(\mu=0\) and \(\sigma=\sqrt{2D}\) this is the diffusion equation, so \(X_t=\sqrt{2D}W_t\) has the density \(\mathcal{N}(0,2Dt)\) (the heat kernel). See Fokker-Planck Equation and Kolmogorov Equations.

The reverse direction is the Feynman-Kac formula, which represents the solution of \(u_t=D\Delta u+c(x)u\) as an expectation over Brownian paths (see Feynman-Kac Formula). The Black-Scholes PDE is a diffusion equation after a change of variables.

Let \(u\) solve \(u_t=D\Delta u\) on \(\mathbb{R}^n\) with \(u(0,\cdot)=\delta_0\). Then \[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^n}|x|^2u(t,x)~dx=2nDt. \]

Proof. Let \(M(t)=\int|x|^2u~dx\). Differentiating under the integral and integrating by parts twice (boundary terms vanish by Gaussian decay), \[ M'(t)=D\int|x|^2\Delta u~dx=D\int\Delta(|x|^2)u~dx=2nD\int u~dx=2nD, \] since \(\Delta|x|^2=2n\) and the total mass is \(1\). With \(M(0)=0\) the claim follows.

Note: The linear growth of the mean square displacement in \(t\) is the signature of ordinary diffusion and is how \(D\) is measured experimentally. The Einstein relation \(D=k_BT/\gamma\) links \(D\) to the temperature \(T\) and the friction coefficient \(\gamma\).

5 Properties

Let \(u_t=\nabla\cdot(D\nabla u)\) with \(D\ge D_{\min}>0\) on a bounded domain.

  1. Conservation: with no-flux boundary conditions \(\vec J\cdot\vec n=0\), the total mass \(\int_\Omega u~dx\) is constant.
  2. Maximum principle: \(\min u_0\le u\le\max u_0\) (with zero source, and absent flux through the boundary or Dirichlet data in this range).
  3. Energy decay: with Dirichlet or no-flux boundaries, \(\frac{d}{dt}\int u^2~dx=-2\int D|\nabla u|^2~dx\le0\).
  4. Irreversibility: the equation is not invariant under \(t\to-t\) and backward problems are ill-posed.
  5. Convergence to equilibrium: with no-flux boundaries \(u\to\bar u\), the mean of \(u_0\), exponentially fast at a rate given by the smallest non-zero Neumann eigenvalue of \(-\nabla\cdot(D\nabla)\).

Proof. (3) Multiply by \(u\) and integrate, using the divergence theorem, \[ \frac12\frac{d}{dt}\int u^2=\int u\nabla\cdot(D\nabla u) =\int_{\partial\Omega}uD\partial_\nu u~dS-\int D|\nabla u|^2, \] and the boundary term vanishes for either type of boundary condition.

(5) Expand \(u=\sum_ka_k(t)\varphi_k\) in eigenfunctions of the Neumann problem. Then \(a_k'=-\mu_ka_k\), so \(a_k(t)=a_k(0)e^{-\mu_kt}\). The first eigenvalue is \(\mu_0=0\) with \(\varphi_0\) constant, which carries the mean, and all other modes decay at rate at least \(\mu_1>0\).

Note: Property 4 is the mathematical expression of the second law of thermodynamics. Mode \(k\) decays at rate \(\mu_k\sim k^2\), so running the equation backwards would amplify high frequencies without bound, which is why deblurring (the inverse of diffusion) is an ill-posed problem.

6 Solutions

6.1 Similarity (Scaling) Solution

The equation \(u_t=Du_{xx}\) is invariant under \(x\to\lambda x\), \(t\to\lambda^2t\). It therefore admits solutions of the form \[ u(t,x)=t^{-1/2}\,g\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt t}\right). \] Substituting \(\xi=x/\sqrt t\) gives the ODE \(Dg''+\tfrac12\xi g'+\tfrac12g=0\), that is \((Dg'+\tfrac12\xi g)'=0\), whose integrable solution is \(g(\xi)=Ce^{-\xi^2/4D}\) and recovers the heat kernel \[ \Phi(t,x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}e^{-x^2/4Dt}. \]

6.2 Fourier Transform

On \(\mathbb{R}^n\) take the Fourier transform \(\hat u(t,\xi)=\int e^{-i\xi\cdot x}u(t,x)~dx\) so that \(\Delta\to-|\xi|^2\). The equation becomes the ODE \(\hat u_t=-D|\xi|^2\hat u\) with solution \(\hat u=e^{-D|\xi|^2t}\hat u_0\). Inverting, \[ u(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\frac{1}{(4\pi Dt)^{n/2}}e^{-|x-y|^2/4Dt}u_0(y)~dy. \] Each Fourier mode decays at its own rate \(D|\xi|^2\), which makes the smoothing property transparent: high-frequency content is damped fastest.

6.3 Bounded Domain

For \(u_t=Du_{xx}\) on \((0,L)\) with Neumann (insulated) ends the eigenfunctions are \(\cos(n\pi x/L)\) and \[ u(t,x)=\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_ne^{-D(n\pi/L)^2t}\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}, \] with \(a_n=\frac2L\int_0^Lu_0\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}~dx\). As \(t\to\infty\) \(u\to a_0/2\), the average of \(u_0\), which is the equilibrium.

The slowest non-trivial mode decays like \(e^{-D\pi^2t/L^2}\), so the equilibration time scales as \(L^2/(D\pi^2)\). Doubling the length of the domain multiplies the time by four. This is why diffusion is efficient at cell scales (microseconds over micrometres) but hopelessly slow at macroscopic scales (years over metres for a typical liquid).

6.4 Numerical Solution

The explicit (forward Euler, central difference) scheme in one dimension is \[ u_i^{n+1}=u_i^n+r\left(u_{i+1}^n-2u_i^n+u_{i-1}^n\right),\qquad r=\frac{D\Delta t}{\Delta x^2}. \] It is stable only if \(r\le\frac12\), which is a severe restriction on the time step. The implicit (backward Euler) and Crank-Nicolson schemes are unconditionally stable and require solving a tridiagonal system at each step. See Finite-Difference Methods.
