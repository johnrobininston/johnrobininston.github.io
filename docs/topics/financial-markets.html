1 Financial Asset

Quantitative Finance is a branch of applied mathematics that focuses on mathematical models for financial markets and the prices of assets.

A financial asset is a tradable resource that has value and can be bought, sold, or exchanged.

A financial asset can be tangible, such as a stock certificate or a bond, or intangible, such as a digital representation of ownership in a company or a cryptocurrency.

Liquidity refers to the ease with which an asset can be bought or sold in the market without significantly affecting its price. Highly liquid assets can be quickly converted to cash, while illiquid assets may take longer to sell and may require a discount to their market value.

1.1 Equities

A stock (or equity) represents ownership in a company. The holder is entitled to a share of the company’s distributed profits (dividends) and usually has voting rights. The price of one share at time \(t\) is written \(S_t\).

1.2 Fixed Income

A bond is a debt security issued by a government or corporation. The issuer pays interest (coupons) to the holder over a specified period and repays the face value at maturity. A zero-coupon bond pays no coupons and only returns the face value at maturity \(T\); its price at time \(t\) is written \(p(t,T)\). See Interest Rates and Bond Market Model.

1.3 Funds

A fund pools money from many investors into a portfolio. A mutual fund is priced once a day at its net asset value (NAV), while an exchange-traded fund (ETF) is bought and sold on an exchange throughout the day. Many funds aim to replicate an index (see below).

1.4 Commodities

Commodities are physical goods such as gold, oil or agricultural products. They are often traded through futures contracts rather than by taking delivery of the physical good.

1.5 Currencies

In the foreign exchange (forex or FX) market, one currency is traded for another. A quote such as EUR/USD \(= 1.10\) means that one euro costs \(1.10\) US dollars. The FX market is one of the largest and most liquid markets and trades mostly over the counter.

1.6 Digital Assets

Cryptocurrencies, such as Bitcoin, are digital assets recorded on a blockchain. They trade on exchanges around the clock and are typically much more volatile than traditional assets.

1.7 Derivatives

Derivatives are financial instruments whose value is derived from an underlying asset, index or rate. See [derivatives].

2 Indices and Benchmarks

A financial index measures the performance of a basket of assets, combining their prices into a single number.

An index is not itself tradable, but it serves as a benchmark against which the performance of a fund or portfolio is compared. The method used to combine the prices is called the weighting:

  • Price-weighted: the index is the sum of the constituent prices divided by a divisor \(D\), \[ I_t = \frac{1}{D}\sum_{k} S^{(k)}_t. \] An example is the Dow Jones Industrial Average.
  • Market-capitalization-weighted: each constituent has a weight proportional to its market capitalization (share price times number of shares outstanding), so larger companies move the index more. An example is the S&P 500, which tracks 500 large US companies.

A price index only reflects changes in prices, while a total return index also assumes that dividends are reinvested. Index funds and ETFs replicate an index by holding its constituents, which is how investors gain exposure to an index.

Some indices measure a quantity other than a price. The VIX tracks the expected volatility of the S&P 500 implied by option prices, and is often called the “fear gauge”. See Volatility Surfaces.

3 Trading Venues

Assets can be traded in two ways:

  1. Exchange: trading on a centralized, regulated marketplace (for example the NYSE or Nasdaq) with standardized contracts, publicly visible prices and a clearinghouse that guarantees trades.
  2. Over-the-counter (OTC): trading that occurs directly between two parties, without the involvement of an exchange. OTC trading is often used for customized or less liquid assets, such as bonds, swaps and currencies, and exposes each party to the risk that the other defaults (counterparty risk).

Markets are also split by what is being traded. In the primary market an issuer sells new securities to investors (for example, an initial public offering), and the issuer receives the money. In the secondary market investors trade existing securities with each other, and the issuer is not involved.

How prices form and how orders are matched is covered in [[limit-order-books]].

4 Market Participants

Participants are often grouped by their motive for trading:

  • Hedgers use financial instruments to reduce an existing risk, for example an airline fixing the price of future fuel purchases.
  • Speculators take on risk in the hope of profit from a view on future prices.
  • Arbitrageurs look for riskless profit from price discrepancies. See Risk-Neutral Pricing.
  • Market makers quote both a buying (bid) and a selling (ask) price, which provides liquidity to the market.

5 Returns and Prices

Prices of different assets are compared through their returns. Given prices \(S_t\) and \(S_{t+1}\), the simple return and log return over one period are

\[ R_{t+1} = \frac{S_{t+1} - S_t}{S_t}, \qquad R^{\log}_{t+1} = \ln\frac{S_{t+1}}{S_t} = \ln(1 + R_{t+1}). \]

Log returns are additive over time, since \(\ln(S_T/S_0) = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} R^{\log}_{t+1}\), which makes them convenient for modeling.

Future prices are uncertain, so we represent the price of an asset over time by a stochastic process \((S_t)_{t\ge 0}\). The simplest choice is Geometric Brownian Motion,

\[ dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t, \]

which is the model behind Black-Scholes-Merton Model. Under it, log returns are normally distributed.

6 The Risk-Free Asset

Alongside risky assets, models include a risk-free asset, such as a bank account or government bond. Let \(S^0_t\) be the amount in a bank account at time \(t\) with \(S^0_0 = 1\) and short rate \(r_t\). Then

\[ dS^0_t = r_t S^0_t\,dt, \qquad S^0_t = \exp\left(\int_0^t r_s\,ds\right), \]

which reduces to \(S^0_t = e^{rt}\) for a constant rate \(r\). See Interest and Interest Rates.

A risky asset is expected to earn more than the risk-free rate, and the difference is the risk premium. In derivatives pricing we change to the Risk-Neutral Pricing, under which all discounted asset prices are martingales.
