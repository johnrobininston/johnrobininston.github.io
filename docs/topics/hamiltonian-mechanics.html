1 Hamiltonian Mechanics

Hamiltonian mechanics reformulates Lagrangian Mechanics in terms of positions \(\boldsymbol{q}\) and momenta \(\boldsymbol{p}\), replacing the \(n\) second-order Euler-Lagrange equations by \(2n\) first-order equations. It is equivalent to Newton’s laws and to the Lagrangian formulation, and is the natural setting for conservation laws and the Hamilton-Jacobi Equation.

Let \(\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{q},\dot{\boldsymbol{q}},t)\) be the Lagrangian of a system with generalised coordinates \(\boldsymbol{q}=(q_1,\dots,q_n)\). The momenta are \[ p_{i}:=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot q_{i}}. \]

Assume the map \(\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}\mapsto\boldsymbol{p}\) is invertible. The Hamiltonian is the Legendre transform of \(\mathcal{L}\) in the velocities, \[ H(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{p},t)=\sum_{i=1}^np_i\dot q_i-\mathcal{L} (\boldsymbol{q},\dot{\boldsymbol{q}},t), \] with \(\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}\) expressed as a function of \((\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{p},t)\). The space of pairs \((\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{p})\) is the phase space.

The Euler-Lagrange equations are equivalent to \[ \dot q_i=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i},\qquad \dot p_i=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i},\qquad \frac{\partial H}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial t}. \] Moreover, along solutions \(\dfrac{dH}{dt}=\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial t}\), so \(H\) is conserved when the system has no explicit time dependence.

Proof. Differentiating \(H=\sum p_i\dot q_i-\mathcal{L}\) with respect to \(q_i\) at fixed \(p\), the terms involving \(\partial\dot q/\partial q_i\) cancel because \(p_j=\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\dot q_j\): \[ \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial q_i} =-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot q_i}=-\dot p_i, \] using the Euler-Lagrange equation. The same cancellation gives \(\partial H/\partial p_i=\dot q_i\) and \(\partial H/\partial t=-\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial t\). Finally \[ \frac{dH}{dt}=\sum_i\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\dot q_i +\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\dot p_i\right)+\frac{\partial H}{\partial t} =\frac{\partial H}{\partial t}, \] since the bracket is \(-\dot p_i\dot q_i+\dot q_i\dot p_i=0\).

For \(\mathcal{L}=K-U\) with kinetic energy quadratic in the velocities, \(H=K+U\) is the total energy.

For the pendulum with \(\mathcal{L}=\tfrac12ml^2\dot\theta^2+mgl\cos\theta\) we have \(p=ml^2\dot\theta\) and \[ H=p\dot\theta-\mathcal{L}=\frac{p^2}{2ml^2}-mgl\cos\theta. \] Hamilton’s equations are \(\dot\theta=p/(ml^2)\) and \(\dot p=-mgl\sin\theta\), which combine to \(\ddot\theta=-(g/l)\sin\theta\). Since \(H\) does not depend on \(t\), the energy \(H\) is conserved and the orbits in phase space are its level sets.

2 Hamiltonian Matrices

For a system with a quadratic Hamiltonian \(H=\tfrac12\boldsymbol{z}^\top S \boldsymbol{z}\) in \(\boldsymbol{z}=(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{p})\), with \(S\) symmetric, Hamilton’s equations are linear, \(\dot{\boldsymbol{z}}=JS \boldsymbol{z}\), where \[ J=\begin{bmatrix}0_{n}&I_{n}\\-I_{n}&0_{n}\end{bmatrix} \] is skew-symmetric and \(I_n\) is the \(n\times n\) identity. This motivates:

A \(2n\times2n\) matrix \(A\) is a Hamiltonian matrix if \(JA\) is symmetric, equivalently \(JA+A^\top J=0\).

The matrix \(A=JS\) is Hamiltonian since \(JA=J^2S=-S\) is symmetric.

If \(A\) is Hamiltonian then \(A^\top=JAJ\), so \(A\) is similar to \(-A^\top\). Hence the eigenvalues of \(A\) come in pairs \(\{\lambda,-\lambda\}\).

Proof. From \(JA+A^\top J=0\) and \(J^{-1}=-J\) we get \(A^\top=-JAJ^{-1}=JAJ\), hence \(-A^\top=JAJ^{-1}\), so \(A\) is similar to \(-A^\top\). Similar matrices share eigenvalues, and \(A^\top\) has the same eigenvalues as \(A\), so the spectrum of \(A\) equals its negative.

In particular a linear Hamiltonian system cannot be asymptotically stable.

3 The Control Hamiltonian

In Control Theory the word Hamiltonian refers to a function that encodes both the dynamics and the objective of an optimal control problem. It is the instantaneous increment of the Lagrangian of the problem, and the structure is the same as in mechanics: the state \(\boldsymbol{x}\) plays the role of \(\boldsymbol{q}\), the costate \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) the role of \(\boldsymbol{p}\).

3.1 Control Problem

The system has dynamics given by \(n\) first-order ODEs \[ \dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}(t),\boldsymbol{u}(t),t), \qquad \boldsymbol{x}(t_0)=\boldsymbol{x}_0, \] where \(\boldsymbol{x}(t)\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n\) is the state vector and \(\boldsymbol{u}(t)\in\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^r\) is the control vector, with \(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U}\) the admissible sets. For given \(\boldsymbol{x}_0\) and control the solution is a trajectory. The problem is to choose \(\boldsymbol{u}\) to minimise the cost functional \[ J(\boldsymbol{u})=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}L(\boldsymbol{x}(t),\boldsymbol{u}(t),t) ~dt+\phi(\boldsymbol{x}(t_{1})), \] where \(L\) is the running cost and \(\phi\) the terminal cost, subject to the dynamics above.

The control Hamiltonian is \[ H(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},t) =L(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u},t) +\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u},t), \] where \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^n\) is the costate (or adjoint) vector.

This combines the objective and the state equations as in a static Lagrangian, except that the multipliers \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}(t)\) are functions of time rather than constants.

The Hamiltonian combines the instantaneous cost, via \(L\), and the effect of the control on the future, via \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top \boldsymbol{f}\). We want a control that not only keeps the immediate cost low but also drives the system in a direction favourable in the long run, as measured by the costate.

An optimal pair \((\boldsymbol{x}^*,\boldsymbol{u}^*)\) satisfies, for some costate \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}(t)\):

  • State equation (forward in time from \(\boldsymbol{x}(t_0)\)): \[\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{\lambda}} =\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u},t).\]
  • Adjoint equation (backward in time from the terminal condition): \[\dot{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}, \qquad\boldsymbol{\lambda}(t_{1})=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial \boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{x}(t_{1})).\]
  • Optimality condition: the optimal control minimises the Hamiltonian, \[\boldsymbol{u}^*(t)=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}} H(\boldsymbol{x}^*(t),\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda}(t),t).\] For an unconstrained interior solution this reduces to \(\partial H/\partial\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{0}\). For maximisation problems \(\arg\min\) becomes \(\arg\max\).

Since \(\boldsymbol{x}\) has a condition at \(t_0\) and \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) at \(t_1\), this is a two-point boundary value problem. The state and adjoint equations are exactly Hamilton’s equations for \(H\).

3.2 Example: Linear-Quadratic Regulator

Take dynamics \(\dot x=ax+bu\) and cost \(J(u)=\int_0^T\left(\tfrac12qx^2+\tfrac12ru^2\right)dt\) with \(q\ge0\), \(r>0\) and no terminal cost.

Hamiltonian. With scalar costate \(\lambda\), \(H=\tfrac12qx^2+\tfrac12ru^2+\lambda(ax+bu)\).

Optimal control. \(\partial H/\partial u=ru+\lambda b=0\), so \(u^*=-\dfrac{b}{r}\lambda\).

State and adjoint equations. \[ \dot x=ax-\frac{b^2}{r}\lambda,\qquad \dot\lambda=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}=-qx-a\lambda,\quad\lambda(T)=0. \] In the variable \((x,\lambda)\) this linear system has matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}a&-b^2/r\\-q&-a\end{bmatrix}\), which is a Hamiltonian matrix.

Riccati equation. Posing \(\lambda(t)=P(t)x(t)\) and substituting into the adjoint equation gives \[ -\dot P=2aP-\frac{b^2}{r}P^2+q,\qquad P(T)=0, \] and the optimal control takes the feedback form \(u^*=-\dfrac{b}{r}P(t)x(t)\).
