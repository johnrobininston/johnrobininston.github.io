Mean Field Games

Mean field games: the limit of large-population stochastic differential games, the HJB-Fokker-Planck system, existence and uniqueness, mean field control and a linear-quadratic example.
Author

John Robin Inston

Published

May 14, 2026

Modified

Introduction

Consider a large number of agents who compete over time, each choosing a strategy process to optimize an objective functional that depends on the choices of the others. The solutions of such games are Nash equilibria (NE): combinations of strategies such that no agent can improve their outcome by changing their own strategy while the others keep theirs. In stochastic differential games, the states and strategies of the agents are continuous-time stochastic processes, with the dynamics given by coupled stochastic differential equations (SDEs), one for each agent.

The complexity of these games grows rapidly with the number of agents, so equilibria quickly become intractable. However, if the number of agents is very large and the agents are sufficiently homogeneous and interchangeable, we can study the limit as the number of agents tends to infinity. In this limit the game is described by a single representative agent who interacts with the distribution of the population, but whose own actions have no effect on that distribution. This limiting game is called a Mean Field Game (MFG).

MFG theory was introduced by Lasry and Lions in 2006-2007, and independently by Huang, Malhamé and Caines. It stems from mean field models in statistical physics, which describe the behavior of many identical interacting particles. A closely related theory is Mean Field Control (MFC), which is concerned with the optimal control of a whole population by a single decision maker.

The N-Player Game

Take \(N\) agents with states \(X^1,\dots,X^N\) in \(\mathbb{R}^d\). Agent \(i\) controls its own state through a strategy \(\alpha^i\),

\[ dX^i_t = \alpha^i_t\,dt + \sqrt{2\nu}\,dW^i_t, \qquad X^i_0 \sim m_0, \]

where \(W^1,\dots,W^N\) are independent Brownian motions and \(\nu\ge 0\). The agents interact only through the empirical measure of the population

\[ \bar{m}^N_t = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N \delta_{X^j_t}, \]

and agent \(i\) minimizes the cost

\[ J^i(\alpha^1,\dots,\alpha^N) = \mathbb{E}\left[ \int_0^T L\left(X^i_t, \bar{m}^N_t, \alpha^i_t\right)dt + G\left(X^i_T, \bar{m}^N_T\right) \right], \]

where \(L\) is the running cost and \(G\) the terminal cost. Since the agents are exchangeable and each has a small influence on \(\bar{m}^N\), as \(N\to\infty\) the empirical measure is expected to converge to a deterministic flow of measures \(m=(m_t)_{t\in[0,T]}\) (a law of large numbers, or propagation of chaos). This suggests solving, instead of the \(N\)-player game, a single control problem in which the flow \(m\) is fixed.

Mean Field Game

Fixed-Point Formulation

A flow of measures \(m=(m_t)_{t\in[0,T]}\) is a mean field game equilibrium if it is a fixed point of the following two-step procedure.

  1. Optimal control. Given \(m\), the representative agent solves the control problem \[ \min_{\alpha}\ \mathbb{E}\left[ \int_0^T L(X_t, m_t, \alpha_t)\,dt + G(X_T, m_T) \right], \qquad dX_t = \alpha_t\,dt + \sqrt{2\nu}\,dW_t, \quad X_0\sim m_0, \] with optimal strategy \(\alpha^*\) and optimal state \(X^*\).

  2. Consistency. The law of the optimal state reproduces the flow, \(\text{Law}(X^*_t) = m_t\) for all \(t\in[0,T]\).

The representative agent treats \(m\) as given, which is why the equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium of the limit game: no agent can gain by deviating, since a single agent cannot move the distribution. Under suitable conditions, an MFG equilibrium gives an approximate Nash equilibrium of the \(N\)-player game, with an error that vanishes as \(N\to\infty\).

The MFG System

Define the Hamiltonian

\[ H(x,m,p) = \sup_{a\in\mathbb{R}^d}\left\{ -a\cdot p - L(x,m,a) \right\}, \]

which is convex in \(p\). Step 1 is a standard stochastic control problem, so its value function \(u(t,x)\) solves a Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman Equation, and the optimal strategy is the feedback \(\alpha^*(t,x) = -D_pH(x,m_t,Du(t,x))\). In step 2, the law \(m_t\) of the optimally controlled state solves the Fokker-Planck Equation with this drift. Together they give the MFG system.

\[ \begin{cases} -\partial_t u - \nu \Delta u + H(x, m, Du) = 0 & \text{in } \mathbb{R}^d\times (0,T), \\ \partial_t m - \nu \Delta m - \text{div}\left(D_p H(x, m, Du)\,m\right) = 0 & \text{in } \mathbb{R}^d\times (0,T), \\ m(0) = m_0, \quad u(T,x) = G(x, m(T)), \end{cases} \]

where \(\nu\ge 0\) is the diffusion parameter, \(\Delta\) is the Laplacian, \(Du\) is the gradient of \(u\) in \(x\), \(D_pH\) is the gradient of \(H\) in its last variable and \(\text{div}\) is the divergence.

The first equation is the HJB equation for the value function \(u\) and is solved backward in time from the terminal condition. The second is the Fokker-Planck equation for the population density \(m\) and is solved forward in time from the initial condition. The two are coupled, which makes the system a forward-backward problem. There are two structural conditions:

  1. \(H=H(x,m,p)\) is convex in the last variable \(p\), which means the HJB equation is associated with an optimal control problem. Specifically, \(u\) is the value function of a representative agent.

  2. \(m_0\) (and therefore \(m_t\)) is the density of a probability measure, describing the evolution of a population (continuum) of agents.

Existence and Uniqueness

Existence of solutions to the MFG system is proved by a fixed-point argument on the flow \(m\) (Lasry and Lions), under regularity and growth conditions on \(H\) and \(G\), in particular when \(\nu>0\). Uniqueness is subtler and requires a monotonicity condition. Suppose the cost splits as \(L(x,m,a) = L_0(x,a) + F(x,m)\) with \(L_0\) strictly convex in \(a\), and that \(F\) and \(G\) are monotone in the sense that

\[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \left( F(x,m_1) - F(x,m_2) \right) d(m_1 - m_2)(x) \ge 0 \]

for all probability measures \(m_1,m_2\), and likewise for \(G\). Then the MFG system has at most one solution. Monotonicity means that agents prefer to avoid crowded states, as in congestion models. Without it, several equilibria may coexist, for example when agents are attracted to crowds.

Mean Field Control

In a mean field control (MFC) problem a single decision maker chooses the strategy of the whole population, so the control problem accounts for the effect of the strategy on the distribution,

\[ \min_{\alpha}\ \mathbb{E}\left[ \int_0^T L(X_t, \mu_t, \alpha_t)\,dt + G(X_T, \mu_T) \right], \qquad \mu_t = \text{Law}(X_t). \]

In an MFG, the representative agent takes the flow \(m\) as given and then requires consistency (a fixed point). In MFC, the distribution is optimized over (an optimization over flows of measures, also called McKean-Vlasov control). The two problems have different solutions in general. MFG equilibria describe selfish, decentralized agents, while MFC describes a social optimum, and the gap between them is the cost of non-cooperation.

Example: Linear-Quadratic MFG

Take \(d=1\), running cost \(L(x,m,a)=\frac12 a^2 + \frac{c}{2}(x-\kappa\bar{m}_t)^2\) with \(c>0\), where \(\bar{m}_t=\int x\,dm_t\) is the mean of the population, and no terminal cost, \(G=0\). Agents pay for effort and are penalized for deviating from a multiple \(\kappa\) of the average state. Then \(H(x,m,p)=\frac12p^2-\frac{c}{2}(x-\kappa\bar{m})^2\) and the HJB equation is

\[ -\partial_t u - \nu\,\partial_{xx}u + \tfrac12(\partial_x u)^2 - \tfrac{c}{2}(x-\kappa\bar{m}_t)^2 = 0, \qquad u(T,x)=0. \]

Try the quadratic ansatz \(u(t,x)=\frac12P_tx^2+q_tx+r_t\). Matching the coefficients of \(x^2\) and \(x\) gives

\[ P_t' = P_t^2 - c,\quad P_T=0, \qquad q_t' = P_tq_t + c\kappa\bar{m}_t,\quad q_T=0, \]

and the Riccati equation has the explicit solution \(P_t=\sqrt{c}\tanh\left(\sqrt{c}\,(T-t)\right)\). The optimal feedback is linear, \(\alpha^*(t,x)=-(P_tx+q_t)\). Taking expectations in the dynamics of the optimal state, the mean of the population solves

\[ \bar{m}_t' = -\left(P_t\bar{m}_t + q_t\right), \qquad \bar{m}_0 = \int x\,dm_0. \]

This is the consistency condition. The pair \((\bar{m},q)\) solves a linear ODE system with \(\bar{m}\) specified at time \(0\) and \(q\) at time \(T\), which is the forward-backward structure of the MFG system in its simplest form. Once it is solved, the equilibrium law \(m_t\) is Gaussian whenever \(m_0\) is.

Applications in Finance

MFG models are used for markets with many interacting participants, for example

  • optimal execution and trade crowding, where traders’ price impact depends on the aggregate trading rate of the population,
  • systemic risk, where banks borrow and lend to each other and their stability depends on the average of the others,
  • herding and the formation of consensus prices in large markets.

See Quantitative Finance, in particular market impact and optimal execution.
