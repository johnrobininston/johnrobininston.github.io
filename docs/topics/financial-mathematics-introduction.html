1 Interest

In finance you never do anything for free and you would always prefer to have money now rather than later. For example, if you are loaning someone money, you would expect to be compensated for the fact that you are giving up the opportunity to use that money for some other purpose. This is the time value of money and the compensation is called interest.

Interest is the cost of borrowing money or the return on investment for lending money. It is typically expressed as a percentage of the principal amount over a specified period of time.

Suppose we loan initial amount (the principal) \(P\) to a borrower to hold for some period of time. To compensate us for the use of our money, the borrower agrees to pay us interest at an agreed rate \(r\). Their interest accrues when the amount of interest earned on an investment is added to the principal. The total amount owed at the end of the loan period is called the future value \(F\) of the loan.

How much interest is earned depends on the rate, number of repayments \(m\) (more typically determined from loan duration \(T\) and repayment frequency \(T/n\)), and whether the interest is simple or compound.

1.1 Simple vs Compound Interest

In simple interest, the interest is calculated only on the principal amount

\[ F_s = P(1 + rm). \]

In compound interest, the interest is calculated on the accrued total (principal + interest)

\[ F_c = P(1 + r)^m. \]

Suppose we loan \(P=\$100\) at a monthly interest rate of \(r=1.5\%\) for \(n=12\) months. The future value of the loan under simple and compound interest is:

\[ \begin{aligned} F_s & = 100(1 + 0.015 \cdot 12) = 100(1 + 0.18) = 100(1.18) = \$118 \\ F_c & = 100(1 + 0.015)^{12} \approx 100(1.195618) \approx \$119.56. \end{aligned} \]

1.2 Annualization

In finance, interest rates are often expressed on an annual basis, even if the interest is compounded more frequently.

The nominal rate \(r^{(m)}\) is a stated annual rate that is compounded \(m\) times per year. It is quoted as a yearly figure, but interest is actually credited every \(1/m\) of a year at the periodic rate

\[ \text{periodic rate} = \frac{r^{(m)}}{m}. \]

The effective annual rate \(r\) is the single once-a-year rate that produces the same growth as the nominal rate over one year. Since \(1\) dollar grows to \(\left(1+\frac{r^{(m)}}{m}\right)^m\) after one year with \(m\) compounding periods,

\[ 1 + r = \left(1 + \frac{r^{(m)}}{m}\right)^m \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r = \left(1 + \frac{r^{(m)}}{m}\right)^m - 1. \]

In North American consumer finance, the effective annual rate is called APY (annual percentage yield) for savings. The nominal rate on loans is called APR (annual percentage rate), which is usually just the periodic rate times the number of periods, so it understates the true cost relative to the effective rate. The process of converting a nomial rate to an effective annual rate is called annualization. The main purpose of annualization is to provide a standardized way to compare interest rates across different financial products and investment opportunities. It allows investors and borrowers to make informed decisions by understanding the true cost or return of a financial product over a one-year period.

Suppose we take out a loan with a nominal interest rate of \(r^{(12)} = 6\%\) compounded monthly. The effective annual rate is given by

\[ r = \left(1 + \frac{0.06}{12}\right)^{ 12} - 1 \approx 0.061678 - 1 \approx 0.061678 \approx 6.17\%. \]

1.3 Continuous Compounding

Continuous compounding is the limit of the nominal-rate setup as the number of compounding periods per year \(m \to \infty\). Interest is effectively credited over infinitesimally small periods, and the effective annual rate approaches an upper limit of \(e^r - 1\).

Start with \(A = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{m}\right)^{mt}\) and take logs:

\[ \ln\frac{A}{P} = mt\,\ln\!\left(1+\frac{r}{m}\right) \;\xrightarrow{\,m\to\infty\,}\; rt, \]

since \(\ln(1+x) \sim x\) as \(x \to 0\). Exponentiating gives

\[ A = P\,e^{rt}. \]

One can reach the same result by applying L’Hôpital’s rule to the logarithm of the growth factor. There is no \(m\) in the formula: a common error is to carry \(n\) into the continuous formula, but \(e^{rt}\) already accounts for infinitely frequent compounding.

In actuarial notation, the continuously compounded rate is called the force of interest \(\delta\). The accumulation function is \(a(t) = e^{\int_0^t \delta(s)\,ds}\), which reduces to \(e^{\delta t}\) when \(\delta\) is constant. Equivalently, \(\delta\) is the logarithmic derivative of the accumulation function:

\[ \delta(t) = \frac{a'(t)}{a(t)}. \]

With a constant force of interest,

\[ 1 + i = e^{\delta}, \qquad \delta = \ln(1+i), \qquad \delta = \lim_{m\to\infty} i^{(m)}.\]

The force of interest is below the effective annual rate but above the effective discount rate. In terms of the nominal rates from before, \(i^{(m)}\) decreases toward \(\delta\) as \(m\) grows:

\[i > i^{(2)} > i^{(4)} > i^{(12)} > \cdots > \delta.\]

Converting a continuous rate to an effective annual rate (APY) is just \(e^{r}-1\). At \(r = 6\%\) this gives about \(6.184\%\). Using the 12% nominal example from earlier: calcfyi

Compounding Effective annual rate
Annual (\(m=1\)) \(12.000\%\)
Monthly (\(m=12\)) \(12.683\%\)
Continuous (\(m\to\infty\)) \(e^{0.12}-1 \approx 12.750\%\)

Most of the gain from more frequent compounding comes by \(m=12\). Daily and continuous compounding differ by under a dollar in one worked example, so continuous compounding is used mainly for mathematical convenience. calcfyi

Investing $1000 at 8% for 5 years gives \(1000\,e^{0.4} \approx \$1491.82\) under continuous compounding, versus $1469.33 with annual compounding. calcfyi

To find a doubling time, solve \(e^{\delta t} = 2\):

\[t = \frac{\ln 2}{\delta}.\]

2 Financial Assets

Financial mathematics is a branch of applied mathematics that focuses on mathematical models for financial markets and the prices of assets.

A financial asset is a tradable resource that has value and can be bought, sold, or exchanged.

A financial asset can be tangible, such as a stock certificate or a bond, or intangible, such as a digital representation of ownership in a company or a cryptocurrency. Some common examples of financial assets include:

  • Stocks: Represent ownership in a company and entitle the holder to a share of the company’s profits.
  • Bonds: Debt securities issued by governments or corporations that pay interest to the holder over a specified period.
  • Commodities: Physical goods such as gold, oil, or agricultural products that can be traded in financial markets.
  • Currencies: Foreign exchange (forex) markets allow for the trading of different currencies.
  • Derivatives: Financial instruments whose value is derived from an underlying asset, index, or rate.

Liquidity refers to the ease with which an asset can be bought or sold in the market without significantly affecting its price. Highly liquid assets can be quickly converted to cash, while illiquid assets may take longer to sell and may require a discount to their market value.

Assets can be traded in two ways:

  1. Over-the-counter (OTC): Trading that occurs directly between two parties, without the involvement of an exchange. OTC trading is often used for customized or less liquid assets.

3 Derivatives

A financial asset is a tradable resource that has value and can be bought, sold, or exchanged.

A financial derivative is a financial instrument whose value is derived from some underlying asset, index, or rate.

Think of a derivative as a contract between two parties that specifies the terms notinf a financial transation based on the performance of the underlying asset.

Below we define some common types of financial derivatives:

  1. Forwards: A forward contract is a customized agreement between two parties to buy or sell an asset at a specified price on a future date. Forwards are traded over-the-counter (OTC) and are not standardized.

  2. Futures: A futures contract is a standardized agreement to buy or sell an asset at a predetermined price on a specified future date. Futures are traded on organized exchanges and are subject to daily settlement.

  3. Options: An option is a contract that gives the holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy (call option) or sell (put option) an asset at a specified price (strike price) on or before a specified date (expiration date).

  4. Swaps: A swap is a derivative contract in which two parties agree to exchange cash flows or other financial instruments over a specified period. Common types of swaps include interest rate swaps and currency swaps.
