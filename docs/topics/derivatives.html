Derivatives

Financial derivatives, options, futures and swaps.
Author

John Robin Inston

Published

October 28, 2026

1 Financial Derivatives

A financial derivative is a financial instrument whose value is derived from some underlying asset, index, or rate.

Think of a derivative as a contract between two parties that specifies the terms of a financial transaction based on the performance of the underlying asset. A contract typically specifies

  • the underlying asset, index or rate,
  • the maturity \(T\), the date at which the contract is settled,
  • the strike price \(K\), a price fixed in advance in the contract.

The party that buys the contract is said to hold a long position and the party that sells it holds a short position. Because the value of the contract depends on the future price of the underlying, which is uncertain, a derivative is also called a contingent claim.

Derivatives are used for

  • hedging: reducing an existing risk, such as locking in a future price,
  • speculation: taking a view on the price of the underlying,
  • leverage: gaining large exposure to the underlying for a small outlay.

2 Types of Derivatives

2.1 Forwards

A forward contract is a customized agreement between two parties to buy or sell an asset at a specified price \(K\) on a future date \(T\). Forwards are traded over-the-counter (OTC) and are not standardized.

The holder of a long forward must buy the asset at time \(T\) for \(K\), so the payoff is \(S_T - K\). No money changes hands at the start, so \(K\) is chosen to make the contract worth zero at time \(0\). For an asset that pays no income and a constant interest rate \(r\), no arbitrage forces \[ K = S_0 e^{rT}, \] since one can replicate the forward by buying the asset today with borrowed money. See Risk-Neutral Pricing.

2.2 Futures

A futures contract is a standardized agreement to buy or sell an asset at a predetermined price on a specified future date. Futures are traded on organized exchanges and are subject to daily settlement.

Daily settlement (marking to market) means that gains and losses are paid each day through a margin account held with the exchange’s clearinghouse, which removes most counterparty risk. With a constant interest rate the futures price and the forward price of a contract coincide.

2.3 Options

An option is a contract that gives the holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy (call option) or sell (put option) an asset at a specified price (the strike price \(K\)) on or before a specified date (the expiration date \(T\)).

The buyer pays the seller an up-front amount called the premium. A European option can only be exercised at the expiration date, while an American option can be exercised at any time before it. See Option Pricing.

2.4 Swaps

A swap is a derivative contract in which two parties agree to exchange cash flows or other financial instruments over a specified period. Common types of swaps include interest rate swaps and currency swaps.

In a fixed-for-floating interest rate swap, one party pays a fixed rate \(K\) on a notional amount \(N\) while the other pays a floating rate (such as SOFR, which has replaced LIBOR) at regular dates. At each date the fixed payer receives \(N\,(L - K)\,\Delta\), where \(L\) is the floating rate and \(\Delta\) the length of the period. See Interest Rates.

3 Payoff Functions

The payoff of a derivative is the amount the holder receives at maturity.

We model the price of the underlying by a stochastic process \((S_t)\), so the payoff is a random variable. For a payoff that depends only on the final price we write \(h(S_T)\), and for a payoff that depends on the whole path we write \(h(S_{[0,T]})\). The payoffs of the contracts above are

Contract Payoff \(h(S_T)\)
Long forward \(S_T - K\)
Short forward \(K - S_T\)
Call option \((S_T - K)^+ = \max(S_T - K, 0)\)
Put option \((K - S_T)^+ = \max(K - S_T, 0)\)
Long call
Buy a call with strike \(K\): \((S_T - K)^+\).
Short call
Sell a call with strike \(K\): \(-(S_T - K)^+\).
Long put
Buy a put with strike \(K\): \((K - S_T)^+\).
Short put
Sell a put with strike \(K\): \(-(K - S_T)^+\).
Long forward
Agree to buy at \(K\): \(S_T - K\).
Short forward
Agree to sell at \(K\): \(K - S_T\).

Payoffs that depend on the path of the underlying are called path-dependent. Examples of these exotic options are

  • Asian options, which depend on the average price, \(h = \left(\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T S_t\,dt - K\right)^+\),
  • barrier options, which depend on whether the price crosses a barrier. See Barrier Options,
  • lookback options, which depend on the maximum or minimum price.

4 Option Spreads and Combinations

Options can be combined to build payoffs that no single option provides. A spread combines options of the same type (all calls or all puts) with different strikes or maturities, and a combination mixes calls and puts. The diagrams show the payoff at maturity. The profit is the payoff minus the net premium paid to set up the position.

Bull call spread
Long a call at \(K_1\), short a call at \(K_2 > K_1\): \((S_T - K_1)^+ - (S_T - K_2)^+\).
Bear put spread
Long a put at \(K_2\), short a put at \(K_1 < K_2\): \((K_2 - S_T)^+ - (K_1 - S_T)^+\).
Bull put spread
Long a put at \(K_1\), short a put at \(K_2 > K_1\): \((K_1 - S_T)^+ - (K_2 - S_T)^+\).
Bear call spread
Short a call at \(K_1\), long a call at \(K_2 > K_1\): \(-(S_T - K_1)^+ + (S_T - K_2)^+\).
Long straddle
Long a call and a put at \(K\): \(|S_T - K|\).
Long strangle
Long a put at \(K_1\) and a call at \(K_2 > K_1\): \((K_1 - S_T)^+ + (S_T - K_2)^+\).
Long butterfly
Long calls at \(K_1\) and \(K_3\), short two calls at \(K_2 = (K_1 + K_3)/2\).
Iron condor
Long a put at \(K_1\), short a put at \(K_2\), short a call at \(K_3\), long a call at \(K_4\).

4.1 Vertical Spreads

A vertical spread holds a long and a short option of the same type and maturity at two strikes \(K_1 < K_2\). The payoff is bounded, so the spread limits both the cost and the possible gain compared with a single option.

  • A bull spread profits when the underlying rises. It can be built from calls (the bull call spread, \((S_T - K_1)^+ - (S_T - K_2)^+\)) or from puts (the bull put spread).
  • A bear spread profits when the underlying falls. It can be built from puts (the bear put spread) or from calls (the bear call spread).

4.2 Straddles and Strangles

A straddle holds a call and a put with the same strike, and a strangle holds an out-of-the-money put and call with different strikes. Both gain when the underlying moves far in either direction, so they are bets on large price moves (high volatility) rather than on direction.

4.3 Butterfly and Condor

A butterfly with strikes \(K_1 < K_2 < K_3\) and \(K_2 = (K_1 + K_3)/2\) has payoff

\[ (S_T - K_1)^+ - 2(S_T - K_2)^+ + (S_T - K_3)^+, \]

a tent that peaks at \(K_2\) with height \(K_2 - K_1\). It profits when the underlying ends close to \(K_2\). The iron condor earns a premium and loses a limited amount if the underlying moves outside \([K_1, K_4]\).

As the spacing between the strikes shrinks, the cost of a butterfly approaches a second difference of call prices in the strike. This is the idea behind the Breeden-Litzenberger Formula, and more generally the Carr-Madan Formula writes any European payoff as a combination of calls and puts across all strikes.

5 Pricing

The payoff tells us what the holder receives, but not what the contract is worth today. The principle of no arbitrage determines the fair price. Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) with a constant interest rate \(r\), the price at time \(0\) is the discounted expected payoff \[ e^{-rT}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[h(S_T)]. \] This is developed in Option Pricing.
