1 Derivatives Pricing

In Financial Mathematics a financial asset is a non-physical asset that represents a claim to ownership or future cash flows. A derivative or a contingency claim is a subset of financial assets that derive their value from other financial assets, e.g. financial contracts such as options which give their owner the right (but not the obligation) to purchase / sell assets at some future time \(T\) (known as maturity) for so pre-specified \(K\) (known as the strike price). The payoff of a derivative is the amount the holder receives at maturity and is generally given by some function of the underlying asset price, either at maturity (such as a European option) or of the process from inception to maturity (such as a Asian option).

To formalize our notation we typically denote the asset price process by some continuous [[stochastic-process|stochastic process]] \((S_{t})\) and the payoff function by \(g(S_{[t_{0},T]})\) which is a random variable. The key idea of derivatives pricing is the principle of no arbitrage which states that we cannot have profit without risk. Therefore, in order to determine the fair price of the derivative we need to be able to describe the dynamics of the underlying process \((S_{t})\) and compute its expected payoff which (once adjusted for the time-value of money and the buy-side risk) will give us the price of the contract.

1.1 2.2 Black-Scholes-Merton Model

First we consider the Black-Scholes-Merton model for describing the dynamics of the underlying asset price.

The Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM) model considers a market consisting of 2 assets: a risk-free asset (e.g. a bond) \((B_{t})\); and a risky asset (e.g. a stock) \((S_{t})\), with dynamics given by given by Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM) \[ \begin{align} dB_{t} & = rBtdt\tag{1}\\dS_{t} & =\mu S_{t}dt+\sigma S_{t}dW_{t},\tag{2}\end{align} \] where \(r\) is the risk-free interest rate and \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are the drift and diffusion component of the stochastic differential equation (SDE).

We note that \((1)\) is just an ODE and has the solution \[ B_{t}=B_{0}e^{rt},\tag{3} \] and the solution to GBM \((2)\) is \[ S_{t}=S_{0}e^{(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2})t+\sigma W_{t}}.\tag{4} \]

1.1.1 2.2.1 BSM Model: Risk-Neutral Measure

References: Girsanov's Theorem, Radon-Nikodym Derivative.

Stochastic processes evolve under probability measures and so in the real world we can think of the asset price \((S_{t})\) evolving under some real-world measure \(\mathbb{P}\) with drift terms reflecting the preference of investors recieving larger returns for riskier assets. Thus the arbitrage-free price of a financial derivative is not equal to its discounted expected payoff since one has to compensate the buyers for the risk.

If we assume the investor is risk-neutral, he has no positive or negative preference on taking any risk thus the price of the derivative is the expected payoff. We can therefore construct a risk-free measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) ensuring that the discounted stock price \((e^{-rt}S_{t})\) is a martingale using Girsanov's Theorem, where the resulting drift term in the dynamics of \((S_{t})\) will be \(rS_{t}\) and all other extra drift terms shall be absorbed into the the diffusion term.

The asset price process dynamics are therefore \[ \begin{align} dS_{t} & = rS_{t}dt+((\mu-r)S_{t}dt + \sigma S_{t}dW^{\mathbb{P}}_{t}) \\ & =rS_{t}dt+\sigma S_{t}\left( \frac{{\mu-r}}{\sigma}dt+dW_{t} \right) \\ & =rS_{t}dt+\sigma S_{t}dW_{t}^{\mathbb{Q}} \end{align} \] where \(dW_{t}^\mathbb{Q}=\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma}dt+dW_{t}\) is a BM under \(\mathbb{Q}\). Girsanov's Theorem enables us to find the connection between \(\mathbb{P}\) and \(\mathbb{Q}\). We write \(L_{t}=-\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma}W_{t}\) such that \(\langle W,L\rangle_{t}=-\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma}t,\) then \[ W_{t}^\mathbb{Q}=W_{t}-\langle W,L\rangle_{t} \] and so by Girsanov the Radon-Nikodym Derivative is given by \[ \frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}=\exp\left\{ L_{T}-\frac{1}{2}\langle L,L\rangle_{T} \right\}=\exp\left\{ - \frac{{\mu-r}}{\sigma}W_{T}- \frac{1}{2}\left( \frac{{\mu-r}}{\sigma} \right)^2T \right\}. \] It is simple to check that the RN derivative has expectation 1 so its not only a local martingale but also a martingale, which satisfies the condition of Girsanov’s theorem. In this risk neutral world, the price of the option at time 0 is just the discounted expected payoff \[ \exp\{-rT\}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[h(S_{T})]. \] #### 2.3.1 BSM Model: PDE Approach

The idea is that one can build a self-financing replicating portfolio using stock and bond which has exactly the same payoff as the option. Assuming no arbitrage, the portfolio and the claim must have the same price.

To be specific, assume the self-financing replicating portfolio \(h_{t}\) consists of \(a_{t}\) units of stocks and \(b_t\) units of bond at time \(t\) (assuming the bond has initial price \(1\) at time \(0\)) so the portfolio value process is \[ V_{t}^h=a_{t}S_{t}+b_{t}e^{rt}, \] where \(r\) is the short interest rate. From Itô Formula we have that the change in the value of the portfolio is given by \[ dV_{t}=a_{t}dS_{t}+S_{t}da_{t}+d\langle S,a\rangle_{t}+rb_{t}e^{rt}dt+e^{rt}d_{t} \] Inspecting this we see \(S_{t}da_{t}+e^{rt}db_{t}\) denotes the change in the value of the portfolio that is due to the change of the position in stock and bond and \(d\langle S,a\rangle_{t}\) is the cross variation term. By definition, a self-financing portfolio satisfies \[ S_{t}da_{t}+d\langle S,a\rangle_{t}+e^{rt}db_{t}=0. \] Hence, the change in value of the process reduces to \[ \begin{align} dV_{t} & =a_{t}dS_{t}+rb_{t}e^{rt}dt \\ & = a_{t}(\alpha S_{t}dt+\sigma S_{t}dW_{t})+rb_{t}e^{rt}dt\tag{$\star$} \end{align} \] with the assumption that \(V_{t}=u(t,S_{t})\) has the Markovian form with deterministic time-independent feedback function \(u\). Applying Itô Formula we have that \[ \begin{align} dV_{t} & =\partial_{t}udt+\partial_{x}udS_{t}+ \frac{1}{2}\partial_{x x} ud\langle S,S\rangle_{t} \\ & = \left( \partial _{t}u+\mu S_{t}\partial_{x}u+ \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S_{t}^2 \partial_{x x}u \right)dt+\sigma S_{t}\partial_{x}udW_{t} \tag{$\star\star$} \end{align} \] Matching the coefficients of \((\star)\) and \((\star\star)\) we obtain the following system \[ \begin{cases} \sigma a_{t}S_{t}=\sigma S_{t}\partial_{x}u \\ \mu A_{t}S_{t}+rb_{t}e^{rt}=\partial_{t}u+\mu S_{t}\partial_{x}u+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S_{t}^2\partial_{x x}u, \end{cases} \] hence the replicating portfolio \(h_t\) is satisfies \[ \begin{cases} a_{t}=\partial_{x}u(t,S_{t}) \\ b_{t}=\frac{\partial_{t}u(t,S_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S_{t}^2\partial_{x x}u(t,S_{t})}{\mathrm{re}^{rt}}. \end{cases} \] Our only remaining problem is to find the PDS characterization for \(u=u(t,x)\) to solve for \(u\). We recall that the self-financing property requires \[ a_{t}S_{t}+b_{t}e^{rt}=V_{t}=u(t,S_{t}) \] into which we plug in \(a_{t}\) and \(b_{t}\) computed above to get the [[black-scholes-pde-option-pde-bspde]] as a characterization for \(u\) \[ \partial_{t}u+rx\partial_{x}u_+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2x^2\partial_{x x}u-ru=0 \] paired with the natural terminal condition that \[ u(T,x)=h(x), \] to match the payoff of the option. As a result, the option price at time \(0\) is given by \(V_{0}=u(0,S_{0})\).
